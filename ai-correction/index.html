The S&P 500 enters correction — AI stocks lead the fall

In seven straight losing sessions, the world's most-watched stock market fell more than 10% from its record high. The artificial intelligence stocks that led the rally led the fall too — and then, on Monday, they led one of the sharpest rebounds in recent memory. What happened, why it happened, and what comes next.

By Pulse of the Economy · نبض الاقتصاد
Share this story: Share on Facebook Share on X

A brutal Friday

Friday, 20 November 2026, was a bad day for the American stock market — and it capped a bad week. The S&P 500, the broad index that tracks five hundred of America's largest listed companies and is widely seen as the best single measure of the US stock market, fell 3.7% in a single session, sliding 265.44 points to close at 6,910.39, according to Zacks. It was the index's seventh straight losing session, and it took the S&P 500 below 7,000 for the first time since mid-April.

The weekly picture was worse still. The index lost 8.6% over the week, its worst week since April 2025. And measured from its all-time high, the S&P 500 was down 11.9% — more than a tenth of its value gone from the peak set in October.

The technology-heavy Nasdaq had an even harder time. It fell 5.28% on Friday to 22,617.73, according to the Daily Market Report, and lost 10% over the week. That weekly fall wiped out all of the Nasdaq's gains for 2026: as NPR Illinois reported, the index was negative for the year.

At the centre of the selling were the artificial intelligence stocks — the very companies that had powered the market higher. Nvidia, the chipmaker that has become the symbol of the AI boom, fell 14% in a single day on Friday. AMD, its rival, fell 13%. For companies of that size, moves of that scale in a single session are rare, and they were large enough to drag the whole market down with them.

The fear was visible in the market's own fear gauge. The VIX, the index that measures how much volatility traders expect in the weeks ahead, jumped 26.20% to 35.89. When the VIX rises that fast, it means investors are paying up for protection against further falls. Gold, the traditional refuge when markets turn fearful, rose 2.76% to $3,612.30 on the same day.

What "correction" actually means

The phrase used to describe what happened to the S&P 500 was "correction". It is a technical term, but the idea behind it is simple: when an index falls more than 10% from its recent high, it is said to be in correction territory. The S&P 500, down 11.9% from its all-time high, crossed that line on Friday.

The word itself carries a particular meaning. A correction suggests that prices had got ahead of themselves and are now adjusting — correcting — back toward a level that buyers consider reasonable. Markets rarely move in a straight line. After a long run upward, prices can fall quickly, sometimes in a matter of days, as investors reassess what they are willing to pay.

Corrections of 10% are normal. They happen most years. What made this one feel different was the speed. Seven straight losing sessions, a weekly fall of 8.6%, the worst week since April 2025 — the market did not drift lower. It fell hard, and it fell fast.

Why the AI stocks led the fall

The selling was not random. It was concentrated in the artificial intelligence leaders, and the reasons given for it were about the AI boom itself.

The first reason was doubt about AI spending. Enormous sums have been poured into artificial intelligence — into the chips that power it, the data centres that house it, and the companies building it. Investors bid up the shares of the companies at the centre of that spending on the assumption that the money would soon come back as profits. But building AI infrastructure is expensive, and turning it into reliable, large-scale profit takes time. If the payoff takes years rather than months, then prices that assumed a quick payoff were too high. That doubt — about the timing of the return on AI spending — was at the heart of Friday's selling.

The second reason was interest rates. The market has been living with the expectation that rates will stay higher for longer, and higher rates make future profits worth less today. That matters most for the fast-growing technology companies whose valuations rest on profits expected years from now. When rates stay high, those valuations come under pressure first.

And there was a third factor in the background: geopolitical tension. BBN Times reported Friday's plunge to 6,910.39 amid US-Iran tensions, a reminder that markets do not fall in a vacuum. When nerves are already frayed by doubts about AI spending and interest rates, an external shock can turn a bad day into a rout.

That combination — doubts about AI spending, higher-for-longer rates, and geopolitical tension — explains why the fall was led by the AI stocks. They were the market's leaders on the way up, and leaders fall the hardest when confidence turns.

Monday: the rebound

But the story did not end on Friday. Before Monday's trading began, the signs pointed to a recovery. US stock futures were rebounding, with Nasdaq 100 futures up around 3% and S&P 500 futures up around 2%, according to Talkmarkets. Traders who had sold on Friday were, it seemed, ready to buy again.

What followed was more than a recovery. It was a surge. On Monday, the Nasdaq jumped 5.9%, the S&P 500 rose 4.1%, and the Dow Jones gained 2.7%, or roughly 1,300 points, according to Investopedia. Nvidia — the same stock that had collapsed 14% on Friday — soared 30% in a single session, after its board reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive. Bitcoin traded at around $63,800, and gold at around $3,655.

The scale of the reversal was striking. In the space of a weekend, the market went from its worst week since April 2025 to one of its strongest single days in recent memory. The stocks that had led the fall led the rebound too, and Nvidia's 30% jump was the most dramatic single piece of it: a company that had lost 14% in a day gained more than twice that in the next session.

Yet the traders who lived through the week were cautious. Their warning, reported alongside the Monday surge, was that the sell-off may not be over. A single strong day, however strong, does not undo a brutal week. The rebound showed that buyers are still there, and willing to step in at speed. It did not show that the doubts behind the selling — about AI spending, about interest rates — have gone away.

What to watch next

The first thing to watch is whether Monday's gains hold. A rebound that is built upon in the days that follow suggests the buyers who arrived on Monday really are still there. A rebound that fades quickly would suggest the selling pressure of last week has not gone away, and that Friday's fall was the start of something longer rather than a one-week event.

The second thing to watch is the AI leaders themselves. Nvidia's swing — down 14% on Friday, up 30% on Monday — shows how violently sentiment around these companies can change in the space of a weekend. The AI stocks led the market up, led it down last week, and led it back up on Monday. As long as that remains true, what happens to those few companies will continue to shape what happens to the market as a whole.

The third thing to watch is the two questions behind the selling. Will the spending on artificial intelligence pay off, and on what timetable? And will interest rates stay higher for longer? Neither question was answered last week, and neither was answered on Monday. The market will keep voting on both, day by day.

What does this mean for you?

If you own stocks or a pension fund, you felt this week. The S&P 500's 8.6% weekly fall and the Nasdaq's 10% weekly fall fed directly into the value of the funds and retirement savings that track those indexes, and Nvidia's 14% single-day drop was felt by anyone holding the world's most talked-about stock.

The first thing worth knowing is that corrections of 10% are normal. They happen most years. A fall of that size, on its own, is not a sign that something is broken. What made this one unusual was the speed: seven straight down days, the worst week since April 2025, and the most-loved stocks falling the furthest, the fastest.

The second thing worth knowing is that sharp falls and sharp rises often come together. Last week delivered the worst week for US stocks since April 2025. Monday delivered a surge — the Nasdaq up 5.9%, the S&P 500 up 4.1%, Nvidia up 30%. The two events were only days apart. That whiplash is unsettling, and it is a reminder that markets can move violently in both directions, not just one.

And the third thing is the most practical: a week like this one does not give ordinary readers a clear instruction, and anyone who claims it does is overstating what one week can tell us. What it does give is a clearer picture of the argument that will shape markets for some time — whether the vast spending on artificial intelligence will produce the returns investors have been paying for, and how quickly. That question was not settled by Friday's fall, and it was not settled by Monday's surge. It will be settled over months, not days, and it is worth watching without being swept along by either the panic of a bad week or the excitement of a single great day.
