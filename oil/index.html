Brent falls below $90 — lowest in almost three months

Brent crude has fallen below $90 a barrel for the first time in weeks, after a slide that began on Friday and gathered pace on Monday. The reason is simple on the surface — there is more oil around than the market needs right now — but the story underneath is more complicated, because the same region that supplies much of the world's oil is also the source of the risks that keep prices from falling further.

What happened

On Tuesday 24 November 2026, Brent crude was trading at around $89 a barrel. Reuters reported Brent at $88.97 at 0147 GMT, while a wire carried by the Daily Star and Hindu BusinessLine put it at $88.71 at 0510 GMT, and the Financial Express reported $88.90. The small differences between those readings reflect the time of day and the way prices move minute by minute; the picture they all paint is the same. Brent is below $90.

The other main global benchmark, West Texas Intermediate, known as WTI, was trading at around $80 a barrel. Reuters reported WTI at $79.94, and the Financial Express reported $79.89. WTI closed on Monday at $80.39, down 3.62% on the day.

Monday was the day the slide became a story in its own right. Brent posted its lowest close since 31 August on Monday, and the fall has continued into Tuesday. To understand how quickly the mood has changed, it helps to look back just a few days. On Friday 20 November, Brent closed at $92.57. That means Brent is down about 4% since Friday — a sharp move in four calendar days for a commodity that shapes the cost of transport, heating, manufacturing and food in almost every country in the world.

Brent and WTI: a quick guide

If you do not follow oil markets every day, the names can be confusing, so here is the short version. Brent and WTI are the two prices the world watches most closely, and both refer to a particular type of crude oil from a particular place.

Brent is oil from the North Sea, between the United Kingdom and Norway, and it is the benchmark used to price most of the oil traded internationally — the oil that Europe, Asia and much of the rest of the world buys and sells. When news reports say "the oil price" without specifying which one, they usually mean Brent.

WTI, short for West Texas Intermediate, is oil from the United States, and it is the benchmark for the American market. It usually trades at a lower price than Brent, because it reflects supply and demand conditions in the United States rather than the global market, and because moving American oil to buyers overseas costs money. That is why the two prices on Tuesday tell a consistent story at different levels: Brent at around $89, WTI at around $80. Both are falling, and both are telling the market the same thing.

Why $90 matters

Ninety dollars a barrel is not a magic number in any physical sense. No pipeline shuts down at $89.99, and no petrol station reprices the moment Brent crosses a round number. But in markets, round numbers matter because people watch them. Traders, analysts, governments and companies all use them as reference points — a simple way of saying whether oil is expensive or cheap compared with where it has been recently.

When Brent was above $92 on Friday, the conversation was about a market that still looked tight, with prices holding near recent highs. When it falls below $90, the conversation changes. It signals that the balance of the market has shifted, and that sellers are finding it harder to hold prices up. For oil-producing countries, $90 is also a level that matters for their budgets, because much of their government revenue comes from selling oil. A price below $90 does not mean crisis, but it does mean less money coming in than when prices were higher. For oil-consuming countries, the opposite is true: cheaper oil can eventually mean lower costs for fuel, transport and everything that depends on them.

The slide from Friday to Tuesday

The move itself is worth pausing on, because of its speed. Friday's close of $92.57 was the starting point. Monday brought the sharpest part of the fall, with Brent recording its lowest close since 31 August and WTI dropping 3.62% to close at $80.39. By Tuesday morning, Brent was trading around $89 and WTI around $80, with the decline from Friday working out at about 4% for Brent.

A fall of about 4% in a few days is significant for oil. It does not happen because one trader changed their mind. It happens when the market as a whole reassesses the balance between how much oil is available and how much the world is using — and concludes that there is more oil than buyers currently need.

Why prices fell: too much oil

The clearest explanation for the fall comes from the supply side of the market, and from a striking piece of evidence about how much oil is sitting in storage around the world.

A Deutsche Bank research note, reported by Reuters on 24 November 2026, found that global observed liquids inventory rose by 52 million barrels in October. In plain English, that means the world's visible stocks of oil and oil products grew by 52 million barrels in a single month. Oil that is produced but not used has to go somewhere, and when it goes into storage, inventories rise. A rise of that size implies oversupply of 1.7 million barrels a day — in other words, the world was producing about 1.7 million barrels a day more than it was consuming during October.

That is the core of the story. When supply runs ahead of demand, buyers know they do not have to chase the price up to secure what they need. Sellers, faced with full tanks and more oil on the way, become more willing to accept a lower price. Over days and weeks, that pressure pushes the price down — which is what we have seen since Friday.

There was also fresh supply news on the production side. Libya's Sharara pipeline has reopened, with oil flowing to Zawiya storage, according to the chair of the National Oil Corporation, speaking via Reuters on 24 November 2026. Sharara is one of Libya's most important oil fields, and its pipeline carries oil to the coast for export. When it is closed, Libyan oil cannot reach the market. When it reopens, that oil starts flowing again, adding to the supply available to buyers. In a market already dealing with a surplus, even the return of one source of supply adds to the downward pressure on prices.

Taken together, the two pieces of news tell the same story from different angles. The inventory figures show the surplus that has already built up. The Libyan pipeline reopening shows more supply coming back into a market that is already well supplied.

The other side: why the fall stopped where it did

If the supply picture were the whole story, prices might have fallen further and faster. They did not, and the reason is the other force that always hangs over the oil market: the risk that supply could be disrupted suddenly, in the region where so much of the world's oil is produced and shipped.

On Monday, three tankers caught fire after hitting naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz, according to Iran Press TV, via Reuters. The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow waterway between Iran and Oman through which tankers carry oil out of the Gulf to the rest of the world, and any incident there immediately raises fears about the safety of shipping and the security of supply.

Separately, the United States is investigating whether Iran was involved in cyberattacks on as many as 50 energy tankers, according to the Wall Street Journal, via Reuters on 24 November 2026. A cyberattack on a tanker is a different kind of threat from a mine, but it points in the same direction: the vessels that carry the world's oil are facing risks that go beyond ordinary accidents.

These two reports help explain why the fall in prices was limited rather than a rout. Traders who see a supply surplus will sell, pushing prices down. But traders who also see tankers on fire in the Strait of Hormuz and a possible campaign of cyberattacks against energy shipping will hesitate before pushing prices too far down, because a serious disruption to Gulf shipping could suddenly reverse the surplus and leave buyers scrambling for oil. The market is therefore caught between two forces pulling in opposite directions: the surplus pushing prices down, and the security risk holding them up. Tuesday's price, just below $90, is where those two forces have balanced out for now.

What to watch next

The next event that could change the picture is already on the calendar. The full OPEC+ meeting takes place on Sunday 29 November 2026, and it will set production quotas for 2027.

OPEC+ is the group of oil-producing countries that coordinates how much oil its members produce, and its quotas are one of the main tools for managing the balance between supply and demand. When the group meets, the question is always whether it will cut production to support prices, hold production steady, or allow more oil onto the market. Given that the market is currently dealing with a surplus, the decisions taken on Sunday 29 November will be watched closely by everyone who buys, sells or depends on oil — because they will shape how much oil is available in the year ahead.

Until then, the picture remains the one Tuesday's trading has given us: Brent below $90, WTI around $80, a market with more oil than it needs right now, and a set of risks in the Gulf that are keeping prices from falling further.

What does this mean for you?

For most readers, the oil price is not something you check every morning, but it reaches your daily life through several channels, and a fall below $90 is worth understanding rather than worrying about.

The most direct effect is on fuel and transport. Crude oil is the main raw material for petrol and diesel, and when crude gets cheaper, the cost of producing fuel falls. That does not always show up at the pump straight away, and it does not show up by the same amount everywhere, because fuel prices also depend on taxes, refining costs, transport and the decisions of individual retailers. But over time, cheaper crude tends to mean lower fuel costs, and lower fuel costs feed through into the cost of moving goods — the lorries that deliver food to shops, the buses and cars people use to get to work, and the planes that carry passengers and freight.

Cheaper oil can also ease pressure on the cost of living more broadly. Energy is an input into almost everything: manufacturing, farming, heating and transport all use oil directly or indirectly. When energy costs fall, businesses face less pressure to raise their prices, and households may find a little more room in their budgets. None of this happens overnight, and a fall of about 4% since Friday is not large enough on its own to transform anyone's cost of living. But the direction matters: if the surplus persists and prices stay lower, the relief for consumers builds up over weeks and months.

The other side of the coin is what cheaper oil means for the countries and people who depend on selling it. For oil-exporting countries, every dollar off the price of a barrel is revenue that does not arrive. Governments in those countries use oil revenue to fund public services, pay salaries and finance investment, so a sustained fall in prices can put their budgets under strain. The same is true for the workers and communities whose livelihoods depend on the oil industry, from the engineers on offshore platforms to the staff at refineries and export terminals. What feels like relief at the petrol pump in one country can feel like pressure on public finances in another, and both effects are real at the same time.

There is also a balance to keep in mind about the risks described above. The incidents in the Strait of Hormuz are a reminder that the oil market is not just about numbers in storage tanks. It depends on ships moving safely through narrow waterways, and on the political stability of the regions that produce oil. A market that looks comfortable because inventories are high can change quickly if shipping is disrupted, which is why prices did not simply keep falling on Monday and Tuesday despite the surplus.

For ordinary readers, the practical takeaway is simple: watch the direction, not the daily noise. A single day's move of a dollar or two matters less than whether the surplus persists and whether the risks in the Gulf fade or grow. Tuesday's trading tells you the market is weighing both at once.
